Presenting a brief, a deck or a QBR?

Same prep, different room. Nobody in there cares much about the deck. They go after the assumptions under it, and most briefs die on the question the author hoped nobody would ask. Check these five, then tell me about it above and I'll play the room.

POINT: Can you say in one sentence what you want them to decide, and why now?

If it takes two sentences, you have two briefs. And a room can agree with you and still do nothing, so the why-now goes in the same sentence. Before the meeting: Write the one sentence and put it first.

RECEIPTS: For the three claims the argument depends on, do you have evidence that isn't your own team's opinion?

Not every claim. The three that take the recommendation down with them if they're false. Before the meeting: Write the source and the date for each of the three. Cut what only your team believes.

ALTERNATIVE: Have you dealt with the most credible other option, including doing nothing?

Somebody in the room already likes another answer. Before the meeting: Write their alternative in their words, and why it falls short.

HOLE: Do you know the weakest assumption in your own argument, and who in the room will find it?

If you say it first, the argument happens on your terms. If you don't, somebody whose incentives differ from yours will find it, and they won't be gentle. Before the meeting: Put the weakest assumption in the brief yourself.

ASK: Is it completely clear what you need from them today, and who owns the next step?

A shocking number of decks survive thirty slides and end with no decision. Before the meeting: End with what you need, from whom, by when, and who owns the next action.

Who's across the table changes the questions. Finance asks what it costs, what it returns, and which assumption drives the economics. The executive asks why you're telling them, what the decision is, and why now. The tech leader asks what has to be true and what you're hand-waving. The sales leader asks whether a customer actually said they want it, and who pays. The skeptic is whoever's accountable for something you're not, and they know something you don't. Tell me who's in the room and I'll ask what they'd ask.

Questions

Who's answering?
The AI version of Mark: set up to answer the way he would after twenty-plus years in federal sales. Start with the mission, follow the money and the path through primes and partners, arm your champion, deal with the blocker, do something in the next 72 hours. It's not the real Mark, and it says so. He's on the Ask Mark page, twenty minutes at a time.
Does it work for an internal brief or a QBR?
Yes. Tell it what you're presenting, who's in the room and what you need them to decide, and it'll play the room: the point, the receipts, the alternative, the hole and the ask. The five questions are on this page, under presenting a brief. They used to be Brief Check.
Can I trust it?
Trust it like advice from an experienced coach who wasn't in the room. It knows how federal deals actually move, and it'll tell you what to do this week. It doesn't know your customer's org chart or this year's budget line, so check any name, vehicle or number before you repeat it to a customer.
Does anything I type leave my device?
Yes. This page uses AI, unlike the tools. When you press the button, what you typed goes to Google's Gemini through QuotaBird's own server, and the answer comes back here. The server doesn't save it, and QuotaBird never sees it. Don't paste anything that isn't yours to share: nothing classified or controlled unclassified, nothing marked internal, nothing about a customer you wouldn't put in an email to a stranger.
