Deal Check

Is it real, or is it hopium?

Ask these questions before your manager does.

5 taps · about a minute

Customer · Money · Power · Path · Now

Where deals break

Most federal pipeline dies in one of these five places. Usually the seller already knows which one. This just makes you say it out loud.

CUSTOMER: Has anyone said out loud that they want this?

A good meeting doesn't give you a requirement. You want the customer describing the problem in their own words, ideally in writing, and deciding something has to change. If you're the only one who's put the need into words, you might be inventing it. Is there pull, or are you pushing?

MONEY: Where does the funding come from?

Federal money has a name: an appropriation, a program line, an O&M budget, a color of money with a fiscal year attached. "They have budget" with no source is the most common thing a seller believes and can't defend. You can have a requirement, an enthusiastic customer and a contract vehicle, and still no money that can be applied to the buy. The question your manager will ask is who told you, and what is it called.

POWER: Have you met anyone who can make it happen?

Understanding the requirement and controlling the purchase are usually different jobs, often different people, sometimes different buildings. A technical evaluator can love you and still not move a dollar. You're looking for a program manager, a contracting officer or a budget holder, somebody who can make something happen when it gets difficult.

PATH: How would they actually buy it?

Every federal purchase arrives through a mechanism: an existing contract, a GWAC or IDIQ, a task order, a set-aside, a recompete, a sole-source justification. If nobody has named the vehicle and the contracting office, the timeline is guesswork, because acquisition lead time is the timeline. If nobody can tell you how a purchase order actually gets cut, what you have is interest.

NOW: What forces action this year?

Agencies are very good at doing nothing for another year. A forcing function is specific: a contract expiring, a product going end of life, a mandate with a date, an incident, or money that expires September 30. "Q4" isn't a compelling event, and neither is "my manager wants it this quarter." What gets worse for them if they do nothing?

Why not BANT or MEDDIC?

I've used both. They're for working a deal. This is earlier. If one of these five things is missing, I don't care how pretty the MEDDIC sheet is yet.

BANT gets close, but "need" is often something we decided for the customer and "timeline" is whatever date is in CRM. MEDDIC is great once you have a real complex deal. Deal Check is the part before that: do you actually have one?

If you run the review

A pipeline review is mostly a manager asking whether the numbers can survive a few questions. Ask the same five every week and you get something better than another CRM field: a shared way to say where the deal is weak.

  • Ask the same five every time. The consistency is what makes the answers comparable.
  • Let the rep answer before you react. Sort of is an honest answer and should be safe to give.
  • Weakest link first. The worst answer is the one that determines the forecast, not the average.
  • Nothing gets typed into a system. Nobody has to explain later why the pipeline looked bad in writing.

Same job every week: get the hopium out before the forecast call.

Questions

Does anything I enter leave my device?
Your answers stay in your browser. The scoring runs right on this page, so there's no account, no database and nothing sent to your CRM. I count page views with Google Analytics, and that's all that gets sent. If you hit Share, your five answers go into the link so you can send it to somebody. It never asks for the customer or the dollar value, because it doesn't need them and your employer probably wouldn't want them typed anywhere.
How is the score calculated?
Each answer maps to a number (yes 92, sort of 50, no 8), weighted money 26, power 22, customer 20, path 16, now 16. Three caps apply when money, power or customer is a flat no, and no deal with any no can score above 74. Thresholds: healthy from 75, hopium from 55, at risk from 35, not a deal yet below. The number you see on the card is simpler: how many of the five you can defend, with a sort of counting as a half. It's a rough heuristic. Use it to find the hole, and don't set your number with it.
Is this AI?
No. It's a fixed rubric: each answer maps to a number, the numbers are weighted, three caps apply, and thresholds produce the verdict. The weights are visible in the page source. A language model given five words would invent context, and confident wrong advice is worse than none.
What do the four verdicts mean?
Healthy: it survived; bring the proof. Hopium: you believe it, you haven't proved it. At risk: something important is still an assumption. Not a deal yet: this is a conversation, not a deal.
What does Pressure test do?
It plays the manager. Three hard questions drawn from your weakest answer, one at a time, and you say whether you could answer each one. If you can't answer two of three about the money, that deal doesn't belong in commit yet.
